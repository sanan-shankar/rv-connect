The assembly chants still ring in my ears. When I met Mark Lee sir in 1986 in Ojai first time in US, I remember he played a recording of the chants on his tape recorded and said he often listens to them with tears welling in his eyes! On visits to RV as alum, sitting in on the assembly chants is a must for me like many others, how wonderful they sound in that valley accompanied by the metronomic sounds of the woodpecker outside the auditorium! They are part of the magic of that paradise on earth!

Mark Lee sir, decades after leaving RV, played a recording of the assembly chants in Ojai with tears in his eyes; a beautiful, quotable image of what RV meant to those who taught there.

Coming to Rishi Valley, 1982

Capt (Dr) Rohit Shetty·2 December 2025·RVS Alumni WhatsApp

Since quite a few are writing of the time they joined school, here’s my story of the whole affair :))

A long story of how I was plucked out from my cozy kingdom in Coorg to the Neem B house bed next to Chocka.

I did not read much into it , six months earlier , when dad had casually asked me when I got back from my convent school, if I'd like to go to a 'real good school'. I was game. I thought he was referring to the kannada medium school next to ours. I mean, they seemed to be having all the fun, out playing hockey at odd times of the day when no teachers came to take classes for them, while we sat struggling with books and English grammar under the eagle eye of the nuns. To me, that kannada school was definitely a good school !

But it turned out that Dad had a much bigger extravagance planned. A month earlier, he had written to RV (about which he had heard praises from a parent) and RV had called for an interview, and Dad was setting me up nicely. I pretended to be game, attended the interview , humoured Rajesh Dalaal Sir while at it, and then promptly forgot all about it.

And then, it all hit the ceiling, and how! There I was, enjoying my summer holidays at Mom's place in Mangalore, on the 29th of March 1982 ( how do I remember this date, let me explain. Its an annual "bhootha kola" for us. Google it. .Suffice to say, a true blue Tulu(Bunt) from Mangalore believes less in Gods and more in this Bhoota or Daiva. And once a year, ritual is held that goes on all night, and at my Mom's, its held on the 29 March of every year. I shall post some pics here so you all can make sense of it. Anyway, the movie Kantara has made this ritual popular now) and we kids were trying to get to sit somewhere safe (a bit wary of the ferociously dressed daiva) when Mom came upto me with a telegram in hand, saying I'd got "a seat". I said no thanks, and that I'd found one myself. Then I saw the telegram in her hand. It was from my Dad from Coorg (he never came for these religious stuff, saying he had better things to do. But its different now. He personally supervises and conducts it now, and insists and makes sure we all are there every year on mar 29 in mangalore) saying I'd been admitted to RV. Now I was less fearful of the daiva and more fearful of this news. Man! I wasnt going to join any boarding school. NO WAY!

Once we got back to Coorg, I went about my business casually, hoping my parents had forgotten this whole business of Rishi Valley. It was only when May22 came close (thats the day every year that my dear old convent re-opened) and there was no sign of the new text and notebooks coming my way, that I realised that I indeed was changing schools.

The days between May 22 and the Jun 12 were like a daze. I had my plan ready. I would refuse to go to RV at the last moment. That way, I would get to bunk an entire month of my good old convent school, was my plan.The day convent re-opened , I saw my friends walk past my house towards school, I was all thrilled and went and chatted them up. But after two- three days, a distance was developing. I could see them form their own new equations and I was only an unavoidable pit-stop ( my home was on the way to school).

I kind of, tolerated my convent well. I mean, things were really simple. U did not do your homework, you got caned. Simple. If you argued with a nun, you got caned on your calf. Simple. If you were caught speaking Kannada in school (because we were “Engleeees” medium:)) you were made to kneel down with hands up in the air in front of the class. Simple. If you got your simple additions wrong, a nun's knuckles or a thick book would make contact with your head. Simple. Cause and effect.No complications. We were not emotionally scarred for life either, because of the violent punishments by the non-violent nuns. No philosophical mumbo jumbo.A bonus being that I had learnt to work the system to my advantage too, slowly but surely floating to the top of the studious eco-system .

But as the D-day neared, and my parents went about buying/getting stitched the humongous amount of clothes mentioned in the admission letter, and the tailor kept mumbling "which bloody school is this" as he went about stitching my pyjama kurtas and my night suits, and after a frantic search, first in the dictionary and then in all kinds of shops for a "hold-all" ,and my Mom started writing to all near and dear and far and not-so-dear ones that her son was to be admitted to the "Worlds best school', I to my dismay, realised this was getting way out of my hands and it was too late to back out now.

Yet, a ray of hope emerged out of nowhere. An aunt wrote back to Mom saying that the date of joining was an inauspicious day. I was thrilled. Surely my God fearing Mom wouldnt send me to RV now. But no, she had Dad write to RV to change the date of admission by a day. And RV being RV, politely refused. The letter said "we do not believe in this . Since it is an admission process, we advice you to bring your son/daughter/ward on the said date , for admission (this was the letter I frantically searched for today but did not find , but I'm sure i will. It will be there somewhere in my almirah in Coorg :))

My mom promptly did the next best thing. She had it all worked out to the last detail. I was to leave home by evening the day previous to the inauspicious day, to my friend's house a few hundred metres away, and spend the night there. The next morning, parents would start from home, pick me up from there, and off we go, straight to RV!

And thats how it went. But not without some drama. At the friends place the night before I left for RV, his parents were so protective of me, they insisted I sleep with them in their room instead of with my friend! Despite my meek protests, thats how it went. I slept with them, so shy that I did not move from the very edge of the bed, in a clinically supine position :)) i dont know what time it was in the dead of the night that I fell, nose first onto the floor, but it was loud enough for my friend's parents to wake up and fuss over me all over again. My embarrassment was well and truly complete. I never slept a wink after that till morning when my parents came, picked me up, and off we drove. To RV. Right past my dear home as I looked on tearfully.

At RV, it was straight to NeemB and straight onto a letter-writing Chocka on my next bed. And then, to the guest house where Priya’s parents and mine chattered so much in Tulu that I was lulled into the notion that Tulu was the state language of planet RV.

The next day , I bade goodbye to my parents at the place between the TT room and the Reading room (now its a muruku room I believe). I was wearing my ill fitting white kurta pyjamas as my parents and sis drove off, and then I merged into a sea of Asthachal returned white kurta pyjamas. My dad's words "no uniforms in this new school, to nurture individuality" came to mind :))

I walked into the reading room, and for the first time in my life, read the front page of a newspaper instead of the cartoons that I always went for. After dinner, Chocka found me and we walked back together. He was a fan of the school already and kept telling me "Beautiful place. Lots of trees" and I was thinking "Man ! Whats he talking ?? This is a desert !! I come from Coorg !!!"

That night, in contrast to three nights ago when my Mom was by my side and a low ceiling above, here was a Chockalingam from Madras by my side and a high ceiling with clay tiles and 20 others in the dorm. I made my second plan while staring up. "I will just finish these six years and go back to my old school" 😀😀

Four years later, when I was to leave RV, it was the same thing all over again . The senti me wanted to stay on for a few days after the ICSE exams. I actually wrote to my dad saying so. And he did write to the school . I almost expected a reply "Come on ! You dont want to join on time, you dont want to leave on time”

Good day everyone…

Purandar Is There, Why Worry

Capt (Dr) Rohit Shetty·16 November 2025·RVS Alumni WhatsApp

I once went inside Cave rock, in 7th std. All names mentioned are my classmates.. 1986 ICSE-1988 ISC batch . This was my (pathetic) experience ….

Ok RV peeps, since I couldnt have a'Nooner'to match Arti's, I did justice to a couple of 'Nighters', and yes, just as I promised, a story :))

Although I really wanted to write more about those report cards that I posted and its after effects, I shall keep that for some other day, since our (oil)man Lilly was in town :))

Lilly ? Thats the thing about RV nicknames. It always stays. Tulu, Lilly, Petty, Puggy, Spannish, Thambi, Holy, Kumbha or the luckier ones whose names were limited to thier initials, GM, MS , KV , TN or the ones known by their shortened names, like Chocka,Gaddu and Maha, or games teachers like Marker,Asga and PK (whose real names I dont know to this day)or seniors like Fanta, Motte, Loyola,(and Onto that Chatura mentioned today too, though I really cant place him)these names have stuck. But when it came to the young ladies, it was always the full names, surnames, and initials if need be 😀. And hence, Tara.C. Thiagarajan, Vishali.C.Reddy, Pavitha Krishnaswamy, Maya Herzberger etc whose names were taller than their frames :)). Somehow Sivaraman Sivaswamy was an exception, we called him by his full name just to match his frame. Or if Gadela insisted we call him by his name, we would go the other extreme and call him by his full name..Gadela Venkata Ramana Murthy :))

Anyways, in those first few days after we joined RV, when everything is a blur of getting our "kaavaalis and oddhus" right and a hotchpotch of memories, I was told by someone that Lilly,whose admission number was way ahead of mine, almost in single digits😀, was the one from our class who had lived in RV the longest. He had been there in RV ever since Krishnaji had discovered the Big Banyan tree, was my understanding of the whole situation. But I did feel, however beautiful the flower was, it was an unusual name for a boy :))

And thats why, I almost choked on my food and sprayed it out in a violent cough when told in the DH a few days later that Lilly was the short form of Lilliput. And Purandar was called so because he was...short ! (When he joined RV). Now, this Purandar boy towered over me and a few others like Rajesh Venkatraman and Suresh Kumble. In fact, Rajesh Venkatraman and I had a healthy competition going, to avoid pole position of being the shortest in class. This went on till Ghouse joined school a few months later and destroyed all competition. He was practically half MY size :)) . Ghouse became my benchmark almost immediately, I did not want to grow shorter than him (yes, figure that out. Those were days when I thought one could lose height just as one lost weight 😀) And so, one fine day, I asked Purandar how he managed to put on the height after being a Lilliput. He says "hiking". I ask "where". He says "hills all around" I say "Can U take me ?". He says "Sure. I know every hill around here". I believed him, for he was as old as the hills as far as RV was concerned. "Cave Rock" I told him, for that was a rock that stood imposing over us each time we were on the games field. "This Sunday evening" he said.

And that Sunday evening, Purandar was guide, and I was the hiker as we trudged along the narrow path leading to Cave Rock. Rajesh Venkatraman came along too, for he did not want to miss out on the few inches in height that the hike might endow on us :))

Once we reached Cave Rock was when the madness came over us. We wanted to enter the cave. Purandar says with a Gulliveresque flourish unbecoming of a mere Lilliput "No problem, been there, done that. If we have to enter the cave, we enter from the back, and then exit from the front because that way,its easier inside the cave coz its downhill". Man! This boy knew what he was talking about!

And we followed him to the back of the cave. When I saw the constricted entrance, was when the first wave of self-doubt hit me. But then,so what ? Purandar was there. Why worry ? We entered. All three of us. And the insides of the cave had all the ingredients that I had imagined. The cold moist mouldy smell, somewhat softish ground..

But the path inside kept getting narrower and narrower. Till we, though short, had to actually crouch to move ahead. And it was getting darker by the minute.

But ..so what. Purandar was there, why worry ?. He says "Now here's where it gets tricky. Keep to the right wall" And then, bats. And bat droppings all over. And in the faint light, I could see the first signs of nervousness on Purandar's face with a few #confusedexpressions :))

"Or was it the left wall...." he trailed off. Thats it. At that precise moment, panic gripped us.I could smell, along with the smell of bat droppings, a distinct smell of human droppings from Rajesh Venkatraman's direction 😂😂.

And it was at that precise moment that I made a promise to myself, never to blindly follow someone with self -certified experience, ESPECIALLY if the experienced one happens to be an 11 yr old :))

But then there was no turning back. There was no room, for one. I dont know how many of you have gone through that cave, but if you have, you'd remember that at places, we have to crawl through. And when we crawled through, we actually saw animal paw-marks,which I hoped were some dog's, even if large, but which Purandar continues to insist, were bear marks.

"So what,purandar is there. , so what, purandar is there" is what I kept saying to myself. Slowly the chant converted into "Purandara vittala, purandara vittala...(no repeat no connection to Surabhi Vittal 😀)till I felt a whiff of fresh air coming at us from the opposite side. And when we saw the faint light through that tunnel at the other end, it was nothing short of Nirvana :))

Three days ago, as Purandar sat on a parapet wall trying to fit my shoes on his feet, I was thinking,the hike worked. Atleast the feet size matched:))

And when my wife said " Looks like you ordered more beer than usual"I replied, obviously from Random Access Memory,."So what. Its Ok...Purandar is there.Why worry?😀.

Good night everyone. Today was Mark's day out. Rekha's son. Adorable kid. Spent the day with us. No hikes and caves for them though. Mark and Prarthan seem happy with Football, Movies, videogames and Pizza :))

A funny, self-contained caving-dare story (“Purandar is there, why worry”) that lands a callback punchline decades later as a life mantra.

Arrow in the Dark

Capt (Dr) Rohit Shetty·5 December 2025·RVS Alumni WhatsApp

Well, we played that in the mid-eightees. Am still trying to know which sadist batch started it 😀. My story on it…..here goes

*Arrow in the Dark*

We don't associate RV with violence, do we ? RV, from what I remember, was a very quiet place. Asthachal quiet, classrooms quiet(compared to the cacophony i saw later on when I changed school) , even DH quiet (do we all remember the one-minute silence we observed before we started to chomp ?) . In fact, the only collective sound we made was during those chanting sessions in the assembly :))

And of course, during games. And so, its but natural that most of the memories that we have of RV might pertain to the games field. There are games we may never ever have played ever since RV. Kho kho, Dodgeball, Handball, Netball come to mind. And of course, the improvised games like foot baseball (we played that in the kabaddi court. Rules of baseball, but played with a football. Ram was fantastic at it) , French cricket (with a tennis ball and racquet. Hit the ball, and then rotate the racquet around oneself for a run. Maha with his wiry frame was good at that ) Connection hockey ( where a few of us stood at regular intervals , the whole length of the hockey field. The first person hit, the second one connected to it and so on. The ball would change direction slightly and dart at high speeds after every connection. By the time the ball reached the last-but-one guy, the ball had reached a crazy maddening speed and if the second last guy was GM, he wud swing his hockey stick and manage to give the ball that final thrust above its threshold level and it would be flying a good distance in the air at waist level. Now the last guy had to stop that ball to escape being the last guy the next time round. Or pray that someone missed, in which case the boy who missed would be made to go to the end of the line.

Figure that out. We actually played all this! And of course, mixed throw ball, in which Pavitha Krishnaswamy, with her vicious swerving throws, would demoralise the guys so much, a few of us came up with excuses to not actually play with girls :))

But the game that I then held in high esteem, was one that was talked about in hushed tones among the non-senior boys hostel guys ....yep, Dark Room.

Dark room, was played only in the senior boys' hostel. So a few of us who , contrary to convention, had to stay on in our Neem houses even in the 9th(b'coz we were puny and not yet 'ready' for senior boys hostel, was the logic I suppose), had to wait till 10th to get to play Dark Room. And when we finally shifted to the senior hostel, within a few days I started to ask around "So when do we get to play dark room?" And was told "Be patient, ye shall be summoned" or something to that effect.

Few days later, after the late Sunday breakfast, when someone did whisper to me conspiratorially "Darkroom. Today 11 am. Get your blanket and counterpane along",I gulped down my Masala dosa and ran to Trishul house, collected by already folded blanket, yanked the counterpane off my mattress and was present at Kailash house well before time. And of course,wanted to know minor details, like the rules of the game. And was told. "C'mon man just play. There are no rules".

Now what was this about ? Anyways I kind of gathered that it was all about everyone getting into a room made dark by shutting out all light using blankets and counterpanes. And then one had to enter into that room and catch and name the person caught. And one could do anything to avoid getting caught.Anything ? Yes anything. That's the day I realised the devil lay in the details. And when I saw Petty enter the dark room with a freshly wet towel knotted at one end, and swinging it in an arc with water spraying out, as if he was a cowboy about to snare a calf, I realised this whole thing was a bad idea.,

But then, peers. Pressure. I got in. Survival instinct beckoned me almost instantly. And so, in search of safety, i climbed onto that space above the cupboards where our trunks were kept.As i squeezed myself quietly into that space,settled down, and was about to wrap my blanket around myself, I sensed a sudden silence in the room. As I peered down from my perch,(the lights were still on) I saw everyone stare at me inquisitively. And Sanjiv Mohanty asks"So....u plan to jump onto the chap from there?" Everyone giggled. I just shrugged. I wasn't coming down to face that free-for-all, come what may. Everyone then went about their mission,as in rolling bed sheets into slings and whips, shaping blankets into a snare, fluffing pillows to a wallop, nothing short of guerilla warfare. If any RV parent had seen that room then, no chance they'd allow their child to continue at RV :))

And then , lights were off. And the proverbial gloves too. My God, we could make a room real dark! . And then, the bakra entered , and the door quickly closed behind him. Real quiet inside for a few seconds. And then, action. Of the worst kind. I could hear whipping sounds and stings of wet towels, blankets being thrown and then someone lunging and lashing about and calling out name and then pleading "stop hitting . I've already called out the name" It was total horror to me when the lights came on. I looked wide eyed at the heap of guys who had piled on one another and just as they disentangled, everyone agreed that the correct name had been called or whatever. In short, i couldn't make out head or tail of what this game was all about!

And then, repeat. The guy who got caught had to go out and then come back once the room was dark again.

I was scared stiff to even move. So I stayed put on top of the cupborards. Till someone pointed to me that the guy outside already knew I was on top of the cupboards. I quickly climbed down just before the lights went off again, and crouched at the farthest corner I could find. All went quiet again. The chap entered, the door creeked shut and..nothing happened for a while. And just as I was wondering what the hell was happening, a palm . On my head. Caught hold of my hair. Before I could recover from my hair standing on end, he shouted "TULU! This is TULU . And pulled me up by my hair. And then suddenly the room came alive. It felt like the whole of RV was jumping on me. There were boys falling.over each other in the tangle of bed sheets and counterpanes and wet towels. There was some whipping and one loud "Ouch" .

And then, I only remember starting to fall backwards. Just couldn't balance myself b'coz my legs were hopelessly entangled in the whole mess and the whole bunch were falling along with me as if in slow motion. I couldn't do much except let gravity take its course .The first thing that made contact was the concrete edge of the bed. On my lower back. And then the whole bunch fell on me. I thought I was dying.

The pain on my back was excruciating. Even after each one disentangled himself and the lights came on. I did manage to get up and i heard someone say "Tulu is out". I was thinking he meant "conked out"😀. I was ushered outside and told to enter after two mins.

I entered. Not the dark room but the common room which was in the opposite direction. And then I made a quick quiet exit and went back to Trishul and lay down on my bed . I really cant remember what happened after that. I know i skipped lunch, and during dinner too, funnily no one brought up the topic of me deserting the game.

My right of passage had been a disaster. And when someone again said to me a few days later "Dark roo....." I cut him off with a firm "No. Way" . Maybe that was my true right of passage, saying a firm no to something I did not want. As Arti pointed out a little while earlier. "Say no...and face consequences. Simple" :))

Its been 30 yrs ever since, but the only thing that's come close to that crazy game based on the intensity of insanity was something called the wet blanket treatment a few got during Army training, for "not fitting in". A la the code red in a film called "A Few Good Men" (btw loved that movie, not for Tom cruise but for the brilliant Jack Nickolson, but I'm digressing). Its been 30 yrs and my back does play up once in a while and I swear, its at the exact spot where my lumbar made contact with that concrete edge. And I repeat, its been 30 yrs, so could someone kindly enlighten me and the rest who never got to play this wonderful sport, as to what exactly the rules were? Please. I'm waiting :))

What ? You all think I’m making this up ? No way 😀

The Race Between Siddharth and Sanjoy

Capt (Dr) Rohit Shetty·2 May 2026·RVS Alumni WhatsApp

And I have a story about him (and one Sanjoy Mazumdar) too….

Oh what brilliant running, what speed ! In a long distance event ! In four years at RV, if ever I saw pure thrill on those twin football grounds with a cricket pitch neatly linking both, with the cave rock looking on sagely, this was it.

Its not that exciting things hadn’t happened earlier. There would be a senior, Gautam N, running in repeatedly, adjacent to the tennis wall, culminating in a final graceful attempt, landing farthest into the long jump pit, farthest ever at RV . On another sandpit, on the ground that morphed into an athletic arena once a year, there was another senior, a six-footer whose name I cannot recall, who leapt highest over and across a cross bar, finished first, and was granted another oppurtunity to raise the bar, above the RV record. I remember his tears as he crouched in pain in the sandpit, staring at the pole that he had grazed and brought down with him,as it lay, guiltily next to him in the pit.

My classmates were no less either. GM Ashok once threw a cricket ball into a perfect parabola, and once it descended at the other end of the large ground after what seemed an eternity to the seniors, they had to embarrassingly fight for second place. And of course, there was our T.N boy, Dinakar, who ran an 800 metres faster than anybody in RV his age, with classmates setting pace and then disengaging out of the race , one after each lap, like multi-stage rockets sending a satellite into orbit. Frowned upon maybe, but RV has not had that record broken to this day. Of Curved Sticks and Straight Bats

Capt (Dr) Rohit Shetty·15 December 2025·RVS Alumni WhatsApp

*OF CURVED STICKS AND STRAIGHT BATS*

*Dr (Capt) Rohit Shetty*

I was all of 15, with the 10th board exams done and dusted. In the process of changing schools and in effect, a three month break from hostels, at home in Coorg.

The only sport I could play decently was hockey. And the only decent hockey ground in my small town in Coorg was the hockey ground of the govt school. Off I went with hockey stick in hand, hoping to play a bit and get back in touch with friends.

As I reached the ground, I was surprised to find one of the two goal posts missing. I looked around, and saw two men carrying what appeared to be a carpet. They laid it in the centre of the ground, went back to a corner and reappeared pushing a heavy roller and started rolling it to and fro at the centre of the ground for a few minutes, unrolled the mat, and both started padding up. Yes, just the two of them. As I curiously watched, there emerged a trickle of more men, all in predominantly white rigs.

I went the next day too, and the next. It was the same routine.The two would reach the ground before all others,would actually dig and lift out one goalpost, place it in a corner, roll the pitch, place the mat and wickets, and just as they padded up, rest would appear. As I was to find out later, they were the opening batsmen, and well, if they wanted a pitch with even bounce, they had to reach early and do the hardwork.

All of them would play for an hour or so everyday, wind up, place back the goalpost and leave quietly, much before the local star hockey players made their entry in their colourful outfits and I joined them.

Slowly but surely, it was the cricketers, and their enthusiasm and dedication, that fascinated me. Most of them small businessmen, except one or two "heavyweights" who took it upon themselves to buy the costly expendables like the cricket ball, one new ball every three days. The wicketkeeper managed with hockey goalie pads.

By the fourth day, I was hooked. I put my hockey stick aside, and I asked to be made a part of their team. They corrected me and said its not a team, its a club. And welcomed me . Thus it was that I became the youngest member of the CCC, The Coorgshire Cricket Club. (Though a corny name, it gave the Angrezi touch to the Angrezi game I suppose). But not before they made me get a permission letter from my dad along with a nominal membership fee.

What followed was one of the most memorable times of my life. Most of my "clubmates" were hockey players who had changed "gear"( literally ) to cricket. In fact, the eldest member of our club, Mr Halappa, was earlier a star hockey player and during his heydays,had almost made it to the Indian team. It was a treat to watch him bat, tonking and swiping the ball to all parts of the ground in classic hockey style , while his son Arjun Halappa (who was barely six or seven yr old then) dribbled with a tiny hockey stick around us fielders, pretending we were his opponents and flicking his hockey ball onto our shins. Arjun went on to represent India in hockey and also became captain of the national hockey team. I am convinced we the Coorgshire Cricketers and our shins had a major role to play in his success.

From playing passionately, conducting tournaments efficiently the next step. We actually conducted a cricket tournament in that small hockey ground and invited teams from around Coorg and Mysore. Donations were collected, going house to house, for conducting that tournament. People in Coorg have always been generous towards sports and so, we not only conducted that tournament, we even constructed a small pavilion in a corner for visiting teams, with the excess money that we had collected. In fact some teams brought with them "foreign" players, present day IPL style. Foreign, as in league players from Bangalore and a couple of Ranji players too. Arjun Raja and Santhosh Bhavani are two big names I remember.

Drinks breaks saw "golisodas" and end of the matches saw a princely chai and two biscuits. Being the youngest in the club had its advantages...as in extra biscuits. And it had its disadvantages too. I was either twelfth man when the team was playing, or scorer when our team wasn't.

We had our share of pranks too, most of it centering around a pair of identical twins in our team(one a pacer and another a spinner) and using them to confuse our opponents.

That small tournament we conducted had its fallouts. Two or three other cricket teams cropped up, one full of youngsters and aptly called YCC, Youngsters Cricket Club, to whom we would later gladly pass on any extra cricket gears we had.

I have never forgotten Coorgshire Cricketers nor their enthusiasm and passion for the game of cricket, in the midst of a hockey crazy town. That enthusiasm has always stayed with me and even to this day, I am happiest on a cricket field.

Now sadly, it is the game of hockey that is dying. Maybe hockey now requires a dose of the "Coorgshire Cricket Club" .

“Of Curved Sticks and Straight Bats” — a polished, self-authored, bylined essay on discovering cricket in Coorg, the amateur Coorgshire Cricket Club, and a wistful call for hockey's revival.

Valley Re-Discovered

by Udit Chaudhuri

We all long to visit Rishi Valley as old students. And some of us yearn to give something of the RV we've carried with us. First the distance makes a short trip unworthy. Matching others' schedules is another hurdle. College, job and family priorities clash. Finally we wonder "who'll know us relics out there?" by which time we've lost touch with everyone else, getting middle-aged and cynical. The old dream floats away . . . Well, this one came true over 1st - 3rd October '99 and how! You can be part of the second one. Udit Chaudhuri (ISC '72) relates.

". . . Guess who!" said a strange caller from Bangalore on a muggy August evening, as if he saw me daily. I shot in the dark: "From school? You're Shivanand Reddy!" as if he lived next door. Hearing each other first time after December 1972, were we both floored by our responses!

Nostalgia apart, I was keen to visit the school to see:

• How much or what of Rishi Valley education or values had each of us had borne over the years;

• Development work, especially in aforestation, water management and rural education that we heard of;

• What we can give back which is deliverable, without setting ambitious goals or making tall promises.

I have tried to describe all experiences below, but the second needs to be seen to be believed!

Meanwhile a massive manhunt was on. Shivanand and Gowri Shanker on the Net and telephone hunted down everyone in the class or their whereabouts except Shanker Subramaniam Ghoda, Baba (marine engineer; Where the ____ are you, man!) locating 16 in India by mid September. These were: Aruna (nee Dhote, now teaching at RVS and) and husband Shiv, Shivanand Reddy, Shendy, senior communications executive); Anand Reddy, Kodi (brother of Sreelatha Chitti, ISC '70; successful industrialist) Varsha (nee Prabhu, sister of Milind, ISC '69) and Suvarnarekha (Head of Paediatrics at St John's Hospital) all from Bangalore; Gowri Shanker Garry (now an industrialist at Chennai), TK Ramkumar Rom (lawyer, environmentalist and President of civic movement EXNORA), PS Ramdass, Doss (hotelier of Vega fame) Venku Reddy (financial consultant), Sashikala Reddy, Malini Krishnamurthy (nee Anandaram, sister of Raj, ISC '70 and Srikanth ISC '75?) and Prabhakar Reddy (civil contractor - largest owner of L&T-Poclain machines!) from Chennai; Shiavax J Vazifdar variously Shark, Dorrai, Bava (brother of Homi, ISC '67; Successful lawyer forever tied up in 'dozen 'matters'! at HC and SC levels) and yrs truly, Tidu (brother of Shubha, ISC '68 and Itu, ISC '77; Struggling tech copywriter & consultant) from Mumbai; RVL Ramchandra, Sanda (entrepreneur, also in environmental engg) from Hyderabad; Narendra Patwardhan, Naren (brother of Swati, ISC '68; CA and senior financial executive) from Pune. Contacted barely a week ahead, he cancelled all engagements to make it. And instantly recognised Shivanand on the phone after a good 30 years!

This one journey to RVS gave no butterflies in the stomach with each approaching day. No squabbles with parents for more 'grub' and tins to smuggle, no dispute on the choice of clothes and no goading to pack. No book-laden trunks and stuffed hold-alls either. At Bangalore, Shiavax, Narendra and I were duly marinaded and stuffed like pate-geese by Shivanand and Anand Reddy at the KGA Club and we spent the 30th Sep night at Shivanand's flat rolling with laughter, recalling all our foibles. Woken up at 5 am by Mr Venks' chanting (recorded, of course) of Suprabhatam, we set off.

Gliding in Shivanand's Esteem, four of us reached RVS mid-morning, taking the longer but smoother Madras Road. The change in landscape struck us from the Mouth of the Valley. An engineering college threatening to come up. Lion Rock as it was. Car Rock split by quarrying (a sports car?). Grand Canyon half eroded, half greened. And lo! - no sight of the school from as close as the Tamarind Grove! A dense tree cover. Coconut grove, fruit orchard and paddy in fields where only groundnut and millets grew. Signpost missing at its fork, Sericulture Centre adjacent to it. Elementary School now Rural Education Centre, a modern, tile roofed brown-painted brick-walled quadrangular and cottage-sized buildings showing all concepts of solar design and environment-friendly architecture, straight out of Discovery Channel! Bushes, a canal and hard soil under the 2nd and 1st Bridges instead of sand, where we jumped to hide from Dr Iyer, Mr Venks or Naidu Sir at PT time!

The Office is now all hi-tech. Computers, fax machines and hooked to a digital RAX that connects you anywhere in seconds! No Internet connectivity yet, though. Stocks of colour film, should you need any. (I did, the very next day!) A publication unit sells The Book of Chants & Songs as you remember them. Narayanappa's Post Office has moved to its own building. State bank of Mysore has an Extension Counter thereabouts. We stopped at the adjacent Car Park to abandon cars for the next 52 hours. No vehicles or smoking beyond this point, Shivanand warned us all.

Reaching the ageless bouganvillea covered Senior School Building (the same palm tree at the centre of the quadrangle!) at Tea Break, we marched straight into the Staff Room - no ticking off this time! Same chairs but more rows. The big windows at jumping height. But a difference: Strange faces stared agape as thumps, punches and bear hugs were swapped between a somewhat mellow Naidu Sir, an ageless Mishraji and our own Arunakka who fired us for missing the Assembly. As usual, the delay was my fault – not the long route nor breakfast at Woody's. Over cups of tea we were introduced to Dr Sailesh Shirali the principal, Dr A Kumaraswami the Secretary of RV Education Centre, Mr Shailendran the Bursor, Mr Alok Mathur the Junior School Head, Mr Rangaswami heading the Institute of Bird Studies and Natural History as well as all senior staff. It was also a pleasant surprise to see Kartik Kalyanraman from the '77 batch having joined as Health Officer of the Rural Education Centre after about 18 years of service in the Air Force.

Naidu Sir then took charge, marching us around the campus. Aruna insisted on going back to her classes despite 'hints' from her students to 'look after her classmates'.

The new Children's' School building, next to the solid old Junior School is another quadrangular structure with spotlessly clean floors, covered porches for multiple use, neat geometric shapes, ample rooms, corridors and garden, earthy colours all over, neatly arranged functional furniture shining with paint, tidy racks full of neatly arranged and plenty of children's books and educational material. Simple yet elegant in appearance. Sathibai Akka greeted us, remembering all our names and introduced us to all the children. She runs that and the Red House under her total control, nearing eighty years in age, with all the dedication, perfection and discipline as ever.

Moving to the Arts & Crafts dept next door, we saw that stepped up too. The old carpentry shed converted into batik / textile design studio with permanent dyeing facility; old batik room converted into a weaving class with lots of tiny looms; Raju Mama's den expanded into a carpentry workshop with machinery to compete with a small factory; the old pottery and painting areas all covered, equipped with electric wheel, a host of facilities and a crafts/hobbies workshop adjacent with a wood carving and model-making section as well! The output is exceptional, with children conceptualising a variety of furniture, models of windmills, gliders, rubber-propelled airplanes, sculptures, paintings and designed fabrics that can stand up to an art college! There is a team of dedicated staff for each section. I asked them to think of exhibiting the work at places like Bangalore to begin with.

Nearing noon, we walked across the games field, enlarged in size by filling up the rivulet at the foot of Cave Rock Hill. And keeping with Olympic standards. Four tennis courts and basketball courts where throw-ball and ring-tennis were played. Sub-junior field is now Hockey ground, surrounded by more quarters along with the old houses of Mr Venks (now an educational consultant at Chennai) and Naidu Sir, who now stays overnight at Madanapalle with his daughter and grandchildren, being picked up and dropped by the school's Sumo. At the start of the boulder path to Boat Rock stands an international standard indoor games building. The old Swimming Pool is closed. Football and basketball are favourites while cricket is not played as frequently. PT for juniors is discontinued.

Walking from the fields to the DH (Dining Hall) one could see many more pretty villa-type structures - students' Houses and staff quarters what with the greater strength and close student-staff ratio. Yet I could not photograph most of them as there were trees all over. Some with thatched roofs, some emulating adobe huts. The main lawn is still there, lush and full of blossoming plants although not as neatly manicured. And there is the old sun dial, built under Mr NS Ganesan's direction assisted by 5-6 students including me in 1970! Houses/hostels are no more age-wise like our time. There are many more, each accommodating distributed age groups of boys or girls. Mostly named after a flower. Each room occupied by 4-6 students. With space or books, desk for prep and perhaps double the carpet area per student. A remarkable pattern emerges: brickwork, some adobe- emulated construction; less of stone masonry than before, possibly to discourage quarrying and higher labour costs; local material used in proportion to cement and concrete; porches, shades, roofing in pyramid , convoluted or conical shapes - tiled, cement, lime mortar (?) or thatched; quadrangular layout; lawns, flower-beds and rock gardens in each quadrangle; Eucalyptus, gulmohar and hibiscus surrounding all of them and all painted in earthy colours. Indeed (the late) Nari Gandhi left his stamp on Rishi Valley architecture. Total harmony with the landscape. In contrast, New Hostel of our times (now re-named) stands out quite harsh (weren't we right in calling it Central Jail!) but for the surrounding growths of gulmohar and eucalyptus.

We could not help noticing the enhanced use of technology. The old labs all beefed up, even a storey higher. They are supported by computer rooms and AV rooms, including scanning and image editing. I was able to scan and restore an old photograph of our class in RVL Ramchandra's possession and caption it, using the latest Adobe products on a Pentium II –based PC and take laser prints for everyone! Solar absorption panels for hot water cap nearly every hostel. Bicycles and two wheelers are all over the permitted tracks. The laundry is automated. The kitchen is full of processing and handling machinery as are the pantry and bakery - down to a bread-slicing machine! Biogas-fired burners at a number of locations. And although the senior school building looks the same its library has expanded and a large variety of study materials are used. A fleet of Tata Sumos and a Tempo Traveller as against just one school-owned Ambassador (still there!) and Willy's van earlier. Somewhat a picture of ideal rural development.

Another striking sight is the presence of waste bins, separately marked "PLASTICS" all over. Further, at the Guest House there is a placard in each room instructing the use of the right bins for each type of waste, along with an appeal to carry non-degradable wastes back to the city. Part of this stems from the fact that Krishnaji's concern about the human race's responsibility to the bio-environment had, over the years, developed into higher emphasis on environmental education and awareness. The Institute of Bird Studies and Natural History is a culmination of all this. More about this later.

By lunch, Anand Reddy drove in from Bangalore followed by RVL Ramchandra from Hyderabad with wife Devika, children Pawan and Shreya (the three did yeomen's service in photographing us as a group everywhere we went) and the Chennai contingent Gowri, Ramdass, Ramkumar, Malini and Venku – all disappointed that "Kanti" at Madanapalli had closed, hungry, tired but full of gusto. Getting together, each recalled their exploits and like Mr RV Ramanan pronouncing Shivanand and Venku 'con-genital idiots' (whatever it meant) in perfect 'BBC English' for hiding in their lockers at PT time. Late afternoon, Naidu Sir again took us to see the aforestation and watershed development along the range of hills parallel to his old quarters, DH, Asthachal and beyond the Guest House. We had been hearing about it, but to see it in reality, with Naidu Sir's narration was another experience. With some help of students and some labour, he had planted seeds and saplings all over the campus and the hillside, right up to our old Lost Lake and Tree Top Secret. The trees included eucalyptus sandalwood, and drought-resistant varieties like acacia and red sanders, the last of which commands a very high premium in the export market. He had improvised a substitute to the drip irrigation system that worked until there was sufficient transpiration from the all-round growth and rain-water percolation, for self-perpetuating vegetation. He harvested rain- water by building a series of Contour Bunds, running along the strike-line of the hill slope, at close intervals. This prevented run-off.

The Lost Lake became part reservoir, part forest. Now the trees are worth an astronomical sum, having multiplied by natural dispersion. He went on to relate his patently no-nonsense approach in pursuing assistance from the political bigwigs and bureaucrats, reminiscent of "No hands and legs (complaints). I want (you in the) PT field!" All efforts paid off as can be seen from the dense tree cover all around. About 300% increase in biomass is reported, all over the area. And Naidu Sir was awarded the Indira Priyadarshini "Vrikshamitra" in 1997. And the school had earned a good income from the sale of produce from this forest. Naidu Sir also visited our sister schools at Ojai, Oak Grove, Brockwood Park as well as the Chestland Flower Show, Kew Gardens, other gardening and aforestation sites.

As we climbed the hill listening to Naidu Sir we could not but marvel at the visible achievement of this man, against such odds. With minimal inputs, severe set-backs in family life and the additional responsibility of managing the entire Estate. Single-minded devotion and robust pragmatism. I remember those large grey masses of granite for hill slopes surrounding the valley that did make quite an oppressive sight, especially near the end of the long winter-to-summer term. As Naidu Sir spoke, his demeanour changed from the soft-spoken and mellowed to his old drillmaster form, minus the whistle - "Come on, boys! this way!" leading us up the hill and down by different routes. We all had thoughts at the back of our minds on giving something for what we took of Rishi Valley with us. Perhaps this setting triggered off the subsequent concrete steps.

Walking away from the crowd in small groups for a while, a 'whispering conference' started. It became a serious discussion later, over the evening. Another problem we found was that while teachers (and staff?) got free education for their children at school but their means were grossly inadequate for university education. So a trust fund was also mooted for assisting children of staff members with their university education. Yet another but inevitable question came up about the school's presence on the Internet. We felt that both the domain, its content and interactivity should be governed from Rishi Valley itself, given its fabulous computer facilities and archives by which richer content could be created and updated. The school could also have a channel of communication through the Internet. The existing sites could all be linked by an umbrella Website. We decided to take this up with the school administration.

Returning by sun-down, we found the Asthachal place deserted, its observance discontinued for a variety of reasons. We however observed our own Asthachal, sitting at the same place for few minutes.

This aspect of watershed development and conservation work has succeeded in bringing back birds like the Yellow-throated Bulbul to the Valley. It has been documented in detailed by the Institute of Bird studies and Natural History in its publication Rishi Valley: Experiment in Ecological Regeneration and Environmental Education. Yet another experience was the Rural Education Centre, previously the Elementary School. We were driven there and to a 'Satellite School' by Mr Shailendran. The system and its functioning, difficult to explain this briefly, is the brainchild of Mr and Mrs YA Padmanabha Rao, who shifted there from Hyderabad nearly two decades ago. Adapting the concept of assignment cards for each student as in the Western countries, here children were given a host of imaginative assignments in the local language or dialect, relevant to their lives. The teacher would be a "facilitator" to guide the activity of a child making progress at his or her own pace and initiative. Aids were improvised there too, by the teachers. Letters cut out of rubber sheet, for example, so that their shape could be learned by their feel by children, especially those in first generation of literacy. Traditional leather puppets, Bombalatta acting out plays with a lesson, educative toys and games made out of locally available materials. Parents from the village are also encouraged to visit and participate, such as in serving food etc. At the Satellite school near Kurbalakota that we visited, a leather-puppet show was enacted by children. Sports and Rishi Valley — A Historical Perspective

Sunil Thomas (S Thomas)·29 June 2026·RVS Alumni WhatsApp — PDF attachment (00007947-DOC-20250326-WA0010.pdf)

Every year, for seventeen years, I would get my copy of the Journal of the Krishnamurti Schools and read it from cover to cover. The articles would help re-evaluate myself as a teacher each time. I would encounter the first page of the Journal where the several dimensions of education are listed, philosophy, psychology, classroom experience... to contemporary issues. More recently I began to wonder where or how sports would fit into this list. As a student, alumnus and staff, I have been actively involved with and been a keen observer of sports in Rishi Valley School for almost 50 years – hence my curiosity. After all, most of our children spend on an average thirteen hours a week engaged in sport. It always intrigued me as to how it survived eight decades of institutionalised sanction in a form that is in very little variance with that practiced outside the circle of Krishnamurti Schools. Here was an activity that formed an important part of boarding school life in Rishi Valley, and yet had the potential to undermine what are thought to be some of Krishna-ji's key principles of education. In this time, sports, with all its associated failings, and disappointments, did not fall victim to dogmatic or narrow interpretations of the words 'comparison' and 'competition'.

In 1961, students, colleagues and close friends of G. V. Subba Rao came together to pay homage and to subsequently compile a biographical record of his life and achievements in the field of education. The book, “Remembering G. V. Subba Rao” has been extensively referenced for a historical perspective covering the 1930s and 40s.

Dr Radhika Herzberger's essay “Multigrade Instruction at Rishi Valley and Neel Bagh: Philosophical and Historical Perspectives”, has also been referred to quite extensively, to help throw light on Rishi Valley's educational philosophy during the 1950's. A lot of the observations made in the essay have their origins in notes made by F.G Pearce, the Principal of Rishi Valley School during this period.

A third source of information came from alumni and teachers of the School from the 1950s to 1970s. These individuals felt compelled to document their memoirs of a period they remembered with nostalgia and fondness.

All these sources provided insights into how the strong influence of a small number of successive individuals ensured a surprising degree of continuity to the way sports has been played and implemented in Rishi Valley. This continuity was ensured during a time spanning five decades when Krishna-ji's educational philosophy seemed to be understood differently with each change in administration.

The first such individual was G. V. Subba Rao whose involvement and commitment to sport began much before he headed the School in Rishi Valley. He moved from Madras in 1916 to join The Theosophical College in Madanapalle where he taught Economics by day and promoted sports like hockey, football and cricket in the evenings. Tennis and badminton were already established by then, but G. V. Subba Rao felt that field games like hockey, football and cricket, for which he had a special liking, had to be introduced to provide more physical exercise. Fallow agricultural fields were converted into playing grounds to accommodate these newly introduced sports. He involved the students in preparing and marking the fields, and ensured that about 500 out of the 800 students of the Institution were active on the fields every evening.

Later in 1918, Annie Besant formed the Society for the Promotion of National Education, and asked G. V. Subba Rao to return and start the National Theosophical School at Teynampet, Madras. There was an emphasis on the “open air” and outdoors at this school, where games, scouting, nature study, gardening, and craft formed part of the day's timetable. To keep the boys fit, a retired military Havildar would wake up the senior children and younger staff at 5 A.M for physical training exercises. In June 1920, the National Theosophical School shifted to Guindy, Adyar, where G. V. Subba Rao along with his teaching assignment, was given the duties of a games teacher. Any assignment from Annie Besant was considered sacred and the “staff put their heart and soul into the task with a deep sense of dedication”.

G V Subba Rao would personally coach the senior teams in turn, daily. The School hockey team held the District Championship for many years. The team was much sought after to play with, by teams from Madras Medical College, Doveton School-Vepery, St. Bede's etc. Many students were invited to play for club teams like the Madras United Club and the Mylapore Recreational Club. The school clearly had one of the best hockey teams in the city.

Similarly, cricket was also played with enthusiasm. Weekends were spent for longer cricket games. The students were coached by staff and members of the Theosophical Society who had played the game earlier for Central Hindu School and College, Varanasi (governed by BHU). Friendly matches were regularly played against Guindy Engineering College and a few clubs in Madras. Mr H.C Buck, an American college sports coach and physical education instructor, took a special interest in associating with the Guindy School. He later went on to establish the YMCA College of Physical Education at Saidapet, Madras. C. B. Reddy, the Physical Director at The School, KFI and Rishi Valley School did his Physical Education training at this institution.

By one account, in 1931, as part of the exodus to Rishi Valley, only 93 students opted to make the move. By another account, not more than 35 seem to have finally made the move. For ten years Rishi Valley flourished under G. V. Subba Rao's stewardship. His biographers recall that wherever G. V. Subba Rao worked in an administrative capacity, he made games compulsory for all boys and girls of all ages and provided them with the necessary equipment and playground facilities. The biographers also suggest that he believed games provided desirable sportsmanship qualities and good health in all students. Education, according to him, did not come only from books. He believed that all experience can be converted into education. Hence, he had no objection to grant permission to the boys to play cricket matches on a working day if the weather was fine. The only condition he laid down was that all students must be engaged outdoors, either as players, scorers, umpires, or at least as spectators.

Cricket became the ‘Game of the Valley’, just as hockey was the game at Guindy, Adyar. Athletic meets had become an annual feature by then and prize distribution did not form part of the tradition. Much like today, the year-end filled up with extra sports and games activities. The intra-team, intra-house matches in cricket, hockey and football gradually evolved into a very comprehensive and widening activity involving the whole school, “including old students who gravitated happily to their happy home in the Valley”.

G. V. Subba Rao believed in a non-competitive society, one in which co-operation more than competition played its part. If at all there was to be any competition, it should be in the nature of self-competition in which everyone tried to beat his or her own previous record. He seems to have recognised the inevitability of the evil effects of competition creeping in, but put in measures to minimise it by engaging students of similar ability and skills, some kind of competition among equals.

Certain sporting principles seem to have been established by this time. One was meant to play a game in a way that was enjoyable, and for keeping the body fit (thought necessary for a good and functioning mind). Games provided learning situations, much like those that emerged in classrooms. Sport and many other activities were not considered extra-curricular, but more co-curricular in nature. Opportunities in teamwork presented themselves more regularly while playing sports than in academic contexts. Other similar opportunities arose during performances, tree planting, litter picking and land care. In such activities, children developed the ability to be aware and attentive, applying their mind to the immediate. While playing sport, children were encouraged to be fair and not to harbour any bitterness, but to be empathetic and appreciative of each other. The ability to cope with setbacks was developed in children both in class and on the field. Children learnt to cope with and possibly eliminate stress, contradictions and conflict. However, like in all other contexts, these principles could be put to practice only with effective mentoring and under the watchful eye of teachers, in the absence of which the evils of competing against each other could rear their head.

After G. V. Subba Rao's time, the 1940s saw a ten-year period where Rishi Valley School continued under a succession of principals or headmasters. In 1948 Krishna-ji once again formulated his aims for Rishi Valley School, some of which stated that – ‘There will be no fixed authority and no acknowledged leaders. The fundamentals are co-operation, equality and self-sufficiency’. F. Gordon Pearce, deeply moved by such a challenge, was willing to consider the possibility of working in an educational institution based on Krishna-ji's vision.

F. G. Pearce was a seasoned educator with substantial experience in the Indian educational system. His work with the organization of Indian Public Schools, and with the Indian Boy Scouts, was known throughout India and Sri Lanka. He had been the Principal of the Scindia School in Gwalior and, later, was the Director General of Education for the State of Gwalior; between 1925 and 1926 he was a prominent figure on the teaching faculty at Holkar College, Indore. Earlier, he was brought by Annie Besant from Ceylon (Sri Lanka) to build up the Indian Boy Scouts Movement. G. V. Subba Rao had earlier accepted in principle, activities associated with the Scouts Movement as being helpful in the development of the child as a well-integrated, intelligent member of society. He ensured that all the essential disciplines that these activities contributed to, were incorporated into the life at School in the 1930s. S. Venkatachalam who taught in Rishi Valley for close to 20 years, recalls in his memoirs, that, for reasons he did not explain, scouting was not formalised at School.

F. G. Pearce was eager to experiment with the novel educational philosophy. The Foundation that ran the School called itself ‘The Foundation for New Education’. When Rishi Valley reopened on July 17th, 1950 with Gordon Pearce as principal there were fifteen students. With the help of some outstanding teachers, among them David Horsburgh, Sardar Mohemmed, G. Narayan and Mahalakshmy, F. G Pearce attempted to draw his staff voluntarily into a cohesive culture, united in spirit of enquiry and dedicated wholly to the wellbeing of students, in a manner that sought to circumvent the imposition of authority.

Krishna-ji was a radical thinker who held that the aim of education was not to prepare students to become cogs in society's wheel, but to create a new world. He challenged his teachers to enter the rough and tumble of their own and their students' lives to create an educational system which was new. Teachers were meant to take Krishna-ji's central perception further with no short cuts to the continuing awareness he sought to awaken in his audiences. He informed his teachers that all systems and methods were destructive and that an approach needs to be maintained which is pliable and alive, which will help to deal with the student. In Krishna-ji's analysis, societal forces imbed themselves in individuals and find expression through emotions such as envy. He observed that comparison breeds envy, and that the whole of society is based on that structure of envy and acquisitiveness. He also observed that in our daily life, we are all the time comparing, and comparison of any kind is insulting to human dignity.

Krishnamurti held that a good school should have teachers who are ‘capable of understanding freedom and helping the child to grow to its full freedom.’ The underlying assumption here is that individuals, both teachers and students who remain anchored in petty emotions are not free. In bondage to their own pettiness, they will collectively create a school with teachers and students who willingly fit into a corrupt society.

In keeping with the ethos nurtured by G. V. Subba Rao and Krishna-ji's critique of regimented schooling, F. G. Pearce's school continued to disregard paraphernalia that marked successful schooling in modern India, like end-of-term examinations, competitive rankings, prizes, monitors, head boys and head girls, uniforms etc. Classes were organised such that students of different ages but of similar competency levels sat in the same room (a concept commonly borrowed for building teams in sports), whereby each student could move at his or her own pace through a subject mapped by teachers, with the help of multiple books. He did not think that tests were necessarily harmful. He looked at tests as necessary so that the child's progress may be assessed by the teacher and by the child himself. However, he was quick to admit that ‘within the system of class-instruction, tests almost inevitably become competitive, and lead to a struggle for gaining marks and for rivalling others.’ This is an issue the School grapples with even today.

An extract from a book written by a student, Raghunath (Ravi) Natu titled “Delightful Days at Rishi Valley”, best describes the atmosphere prevalent in Rishi Valley School between 1955 and 1959.

“RV was going through a spell of experimentation. Princey (Principal) felt that to encourage competition in the early years robbed children of their creativity and spontaneity. Hence junior classes were not graded by number; instead they were named after constellations – Rohini, Shalini, etc. Classes were held in the classrooms and more often under trees and open places. No concept of homework existed. Yes, there was occasionally a tutorial period, generally in the evening. But this was more to acquire general knowledge through scanning encyclopaedias and other informative books in the library. Life was easy going. Most often we were left to ourselves, learning through games and sports, through PT and carpentry, in cookery and needlework classes, and finally by just lazing around with no particular objective other than to watch the world go by. These were halcyon years and such informal processes were subtly moulding these youngsters to learn about living life on their terms, unfettered and free, the way childhood was meant to be.”

Another student from this period recalls “I was my own judge. I was my own competitor and got to enjoy what I was doing without expecting a reward. It did not matter if someone else was better or worse. There was no rivalry or jealousy. I just had to try and excel! Sport was clean and we indulged in a lot of adventure on our own....”

Under F. G. Pearce, sports flourished along the lines earlier established by G. V. Subba Rao. There is no evidence of any philosophical predicament or constraints put on sports based on Krishna-ji's newly formulated aims for the School. Rather, the presence of Sardar Mohemmed, G. Narayan and S. Venkatachalam only reinforced the School's sports tradition that ensured its continuity for the next 60 years. F. G. Pearce understood what Krishnamurti sought to convey – that the practice of awareness restrained the expression of ambition, competition, and jealousy. This understanding probably extended to sports, where sports itself did not have to fall victim in the quest to dissolve these negative expressions.

Sardar Mohemmed was a sportsman in the traditional sense who excelled in squash, tennis and cricket. He played Ranji Trophy cricket for the Holkar State, won squash championships in Calcutta and was a regular winner of all local tennis tournaments after he joined Rishi Valley School. G. Narayan played tennis with the likes of Ramanathan Krishnan at Loyola College – Madras. S. Venkatachalam was a fast bowler for his college and probably for the first time in the School's history, helped establish cricket, basketball and football coaching as part of the annual sports calendar. Ravi Venkatesan, a student from this time, was on the country's Davis Cup squad for a while.

Ravi Natu (Rishi Valley School, 1955-59) in his book, dedicates entire chapters to “Sports Day” and football (Goal!). The descriptions he so vividly provides, re-enforces the belief that nothing has fundamentally changed since then regarding athletic meets and ‘external’ matches with teams from Madanapalle and Chintamani. He recalls that “A healthy rivalry existed between Madanapalle XI and the RV football team, with the trophy changing hands frequently”. The act of participating in tournaments was quite clearly not considered as unhealthy competition.

Although life in Rishi Valley School was close to idyllic for the students, fissures began to appear within the staff body regarding discipline, order and freedom leading to parental dissatisfaction. Despite Krishna-ji's best efforts to help his teacher body see that contrary beliefs need not pose insurmountable barriers to cooperation, that an alert mind and an inquiring spirit sees beyond fixed conclusions; and despite Pearce's own valiant attempt to be reasonable, he was unable to satisfy the contending attitudes and demands of teachers, parents and trustees. Finally, not able to achieve a balance between reasoned order and Krishna-ji's ideal of freedom, he resigned as Principal in 1958.

Dr Balasundaram, the next Principal, and Dr N. K. A. Iyer together took a personal interest in promoting sport, especially cricket and tennis. Dr. Balasundaram, S. Venkatachalam and Dr D. S. Babu played cricket with children on a regular basis, while Dr. N. K. A. Iyer was a seasoned cricket umpire. Staff and students were expected to witness all matches that were played on weekends. Teams from Kolar, Chintamani, Bangalore, Tirupati, Anantapur, Chittoor, Kalikiri and Madanapalle were regularly invited to play ‘friendlies’ with the School teams.

It was during Dr Balasundaram's tenure that professional NIS coaches trained in Patiala were invited to School for short periods during each term. By this time, cricket, football and basketball coaching became a permanent feature. Waseem-ul-Haq, the cricket coach, visited the School from 1972 to 2010. The School sent its cricket team regularly during the 1970s to play tournaments in Tirupati and Madanapalle. During the 1970s and 80s, many students represented Andhra Pradesh School teams in Cricket and Basketball. Basketball Schoolboy State selections were hosted on our grounds in 1973-74. Two students, Ravishankar Chellam and Noor Mohammed, eventually represented Andhra Pradesh in Ranji Trophy tournaments.

The pattern or system of sustaining various sports, developed during the 1960s and 1970s, and exists even today. Teachers like Dr D. S. Babu, Jayaprakash (JP), V. Srinivasan, and V. Prasad who played their respective games at the University and State levels, performed key roles in anchoring sports along with celebrated Physical Directors like N. S. Naidu and C. Balakrishna Reddy. Balaraj S, a professional tennis marker from the Cubbon courts in Bangalore, coached generations of students between 1967 and 2017. Jayant Tengshe (JT) anchored volleyball in a way that represented a student's ‘true love for the game’.

Today, along the lines of G. V. Subba Rao's method, Rishi Valley School almost mandates that children up to the 8th standard play all the games based on term-wise and weekly schedules. For this age group, cricket, basketball and football coaching is built into the annual program. Between the 9th and 12th standards, children can make certain choices and are encouraged to take up any two sports in a serious manner. By a method of ‘natural selection’, children making the transition to Senior School are expected to understand their interests and abilities regarding sport, just as they make choices and specialise in subjects after the 10th standard.

Despite this long drawn-out process of ‘natural selection’, disappointments regarding exclusion and ‘not making the team’ are unfortunately inevitable in any team endeavour. This short coming is partly addressed by mentoring and expanding opportunities by offering a wide variety of sports and extended facilities. As comparison and competition are known to be fundamental features of habitual and unexamined thought, it is the aim of the educators to help students see things as they are.

PT and games keep a large percentage of students occupied with minimal supervision (at least for the senior students) for close to 2 hours a day. Lately, attempts to experiment with the equilibrium achieved over decades did not quite succeed, as the alternative activities could not be sustained in a meaningful way over time. Some of these initiatives for senior students included making games optional and diversifying the use of PT time into other activities that were quasi-monastic in nature. Strong objections raised by the students themselves led to a reversal, back to the old status quo. With a perceived increase in restlessness and attention related disorders among students, alternatives to sport are constantly being thought of (e.g. mindfulness, yoga, meditation), each of which have great merit if implemented in a sustainable manner with dedicated resources, by weaving them into life in a boarding school.

Krishna-ji has pointed out at one of his talks to the students (December 10, 1979), that the word ‘compete’ means striving together, moving towards something together. To those who are not aware of the etymology, the revelation is at the least, stunning. Can teachers identify situations where competing against each other can be transformed into situations where we seek, endeavour and learn together? He asked if the perception, significance and truth of the word ‘compete’ could be captured instantly. He explains that transformations are simple acts of bringing about some kind of order in daily life. Can sports in Rishi Valley embody a silent but determined effort to achieve an optimal balance between order and freedom, awareness and comparison, co-operation and competition?

Suprabha on the Parallel Bars

Capt (Dr) Rohit Shetty·30 June 2026·RVS Alumni WhatsApp

Since you all mentioned Suprabha, had written about her long ago. Just reposting it here :))

In school, my favourite ever senior wasn’t on a hockey field, though I loved the game and one Prem, a super-senior who played with us, came close.

I was on the swing of the then recently constructed play park (that area with the Jungle Gym and the swings, between the football and the basketball courts) and I had this crazy compulsion of swinging higher and higher and then, when in full swing, flying off the swing and landing at a distance in front (I remember that Gadela would also do this, till his sis saw him once and "gave him properly"). So I was swinging away in a frenzied manner, trying to get the required thrust for take off, and this time it was a perfect one. As I soared through the air, suddenly my heart stopped! A girl appeared out of nowhere and was walking right across where I would be landing! I have no idea how I flew right above her head (scalp with a "boy-cut" I may add, as it was then known) and landed beyond her. She looked at me, confused, then looked at the empty creaking swing, and then back at me, this time wide-eyed, and said "whoa kid! Careful !!". Thats how I met.....

Suprabha Seshan. After that fright, I would meet her there in that place almost everyday. I was fond of the swings, but she came there for a different reason. She would get onto the parellal bars and do stuff that amazed me. She was a gymnast with talent. I’ve always, always felt, she was in the wrong school. She deserved to be professionally trained in gymnastics. In fact, as soon as she appeared everyday, I would stop my silly swinging, and go upto those parellal bars and just watch her, in a manner that would now be considered ogling. I was fascinated with the way she did her routine, right from firming up her trackpants which had a kind of a sling that went round the arch of her feet so that the pants did not gather up around her shins when she did her routine, onto the parellal bars, then onto the non-parellal bars at the far end which I till then thought were extra football and hockey goal posts, because I hadnt seen anyone else use them . She would, kind of, rotate herself on those bars, and the she would jump down, and then do perfect splits. She would arch her back and bring one leg of hers from behind onto the top of her head, the foot looking like a scorpion sting behind her, all the while balancing herself on her other leg. Then she would do side cartwheels (which she taught me later) and front cartwheels ( which she wanted to teach but I threatened to run off😀)

And all the while, she would talk to me, barely panting, and that panting too, only towards the end of her routine. And she would talk on varied stuff, sometimes fitness, sometimes trees, sometimes about Bangalore. She would travel with us in the Bangalore party and I dont really remember much of her thereafter but I do know that she went off to do something at the Brockwood park school, I suppose. She did come back once when we were in our 9th or 10th std, with a guy called George, I did go and say Hi but ...that was about it :))

Years later, when TN Seshan became the Chief Election Commissioner, is when I remembered her again and I did wonder if Suprabha was his daughter. No google then, to find out. But google her now, and she runs some self sustaining farming or something along those lines, somewhere in Kerala.

But...she deserved not to be all that. She deserved to be a gymnast ..

And one convo with her, I will always remember. There was this swimming gala that year and our Tara C T was swimming away with all the awards in all categories, right under the seniors' noses ( before the bees made a mess of it all and attacked us). When I met Suprabha next during her rotations and revolutions, I was gloating to her about how my benchmate (CT sat next to me in class, before she changed sections later) had won the swimming competitions. She said "Ya I know. She is very good. Thats why she should not have participated in all categories. Very wrong". Didnt make sense, then. Thinking back now, however good she was in her gymnastics, with that thought process, she may never have ...won. Ever. In a way we narrowly understand winning.

Meeting Suprabha Seshan (later a renowned conservationist) on the playground swings, watching her gymnastics, and a remembered conversation about what it means to “win.”

A Creative Artist

Harshad Parekh·22 August 2025·private record (WhatsApp export; group unspecified)

I had good contact with Sivarajan in Rishi Valley for many years. We met on a road accidentally, between the dining hall and the old guest house, many times, and we talked about music and art for a long time while standing on the road.

He was a good singer, sitar player and a painter. But he never sang or played the sitar for anyone. Nor did he show his paintings to anyone.

Once I took him up on the meditation hill above the Asthachal hill, my favourite place. I requested him to sing for me. He was in a good mood. He sang a ghazal, "Lagata Nahi Hai Dil Mera," a ghazal attributed to the last Mughal king, Bahadur Shah Zafar. He sang well.

He was very shy of people and suspicious too, with some strange superstitions. He played the sitar in his room, but if he came to know that someone was outside his room listening to the sitar, he would immediately stop it.

If he were smart, he could have earned lots of money by his talents. But he remained isolated in his inner world, which was not happy.

He was a creative artist. He loved little children. But he did not like to talk with adults.

He had lived in Shantiniketan for many years before he joined Rishi Valley School in the late 1980s.
