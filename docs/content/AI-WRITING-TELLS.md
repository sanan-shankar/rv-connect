# Telltale Signs of AI-Written Text (and our house style)

A reference for SattvIQ. Purpose: so everything we publish (website, decks, emails, proposals, docs) reads like a sharp human wrote it, not a language model. These tells shift as models change, so treat this as living. Last updated 2026-06-26.

House rule above all else: **no long dashes anywhere in anything we produce.** Em dash and en dash are both banned. Use a hyphen, a comma, a colon, parentheses, or two sentences.

---

## A. Punctuation and formatting tells

1. **The em dash.** The number-one giveaway as of 2025-2026. Models pepper text with em dashes used as dramatic interrupters. Banned for us entirely.
2. **The en dash in ranges** ("10-15%", "2-3 weeks"). Also a long dash; use a hyphen.
3. **Curly/smart quotes and apostrophes** auto-inserted everywhere with suspicious consistency. Be consistent on purpose, not by autopilot.
4. **The "rule of three" lists**, especially three adjectives or three parallel phrases in one sentence ("fast, reliable, and scalable"). Humans vary; models default to triples. Break the pattern: use two, or four, or one.
5. **Bolded lead-in bullets** where every bullet starts with **a bold phrase** then a colon then an explanation. It is fine occasionally; wall-to-wall it screams template.
6. **Emoji as bullet points** or section markers (checkmark, rocket, sparkles). Avoid in anything external.
7. **Over-tidy structure**: every section the same length, every list exactly three or five items, a heading for everything. Real writing is lumpy.
8. **Title Case Headings For Everything** and a "Conclusion" or "In summary" section that restates what was just said.

## B. Phrase and vocabulary tells

9. **The "not just X, it's Y" / "it's not just X" construction.** A top current tell. (This is also banned in our copy for a separate reason: just say what the thing is.) Variants: "more than just", "isn't merely".
10. **The "X isn't about Y. It's about Z." reframe.** Same family. Avoid.
11. **"Ta-da" reveal phrases**: "But here's the thing", "Here's the truth", "But here's what nobody tells you", "The result?", "The best part?". Manufactured suspense.
12. **Inflated importance words**: delve, leverage, robust, seamless, elevate, unlock, harness, navigate (the complexities of), realm, landscape, tapestry, testament, underscore, pivotal, crucial, vital, ever-evolving, fast-paced, game-changer, cutting-edge, revolutionize, empower, foster, boasts, dive in, embark, journey.
13. **Empty hedging and throat-clearing**: "It is important to note that", "It is worth mentioning", "In today's world", "In the world of X", "When it comes to X", "At the end of the day".
14. **Correlative scaffolding**: "Whether you are A or B...", "From A to B...", "Not only... but also...". Used once it is fine; as a default sentence shape it is a tell.
15. **Hollow conclusions**: "Ultimately,", "In conclusion,", "Overall,", "At its core," followed by a vague summary that adds nothing.
16. **Over-qualified safety language**: "It is essential to consider", "Always consult a professional", "While there are many factors". Reads like a model covering itself.
17. **"Stands as a testament to", "plays a crucial role in", "serves as a", "paves the way for", "in the ever-evolving landscape of".** Pure filler connective tissue.
18. **Praise-then-pivot openings**: "Great question!", "Absolutely!", "Certainly!", "I'd be happy to". Fine in chat, never in published copy.

## C. Structural and rhetorical tells

19. **Perfect balance / false symmetry.** Every pro has a con, every point has a counterpoint, every paragraph is the same weight. Humans have opinions and lean.
20. **No real specifics.** AI text describes categories ("various industries", "a range of benefits", "numerous stakeholders") instead of naming the actual thing, number, person or example. Specificity is the strongest human signal.
21. **Restating the prompt.** Beginning an answer by rephrasing the question back.
22. **Even-handed to a fault.** Refusing to commit to a view, "there are many perspectives", "it depends on your needs".
23. **Smooth but empty transitions.** "Moreover", "Furthermore", "Additionally", "That said", "On the other hand" stacked mechanically.
24. **The summary sentence at the end of every paragraph** that repeats the paragraph's first sentence in different words.
25. **Uniform sentence rhythm.** AI tends toward medium-length, evenly-paced sentences. Humans vary hard: a long winding sentence, then a short one. Like this.

## D. Domain-specific tells for us (EPC / B2B)

26. **Generic benefit-speak**: "streamline your workflows", "drive efficiency", "unlock value", "transform your operations", "boost productivity". An EPC engineer's eyes glaze instantly. Say the concrete thing: "catches a datasheet that disagrees with the H&MB before it reaches the vendor".
27. **Buzzword stacking**: "AI-powered, cloud-native, end-to-end, next-generation platform". One claim, proven, beats five adjectives.
28. **Fake precision**: "increase efficiency by up to 10x" with no basis. We have a real number basis (engineer-hours saved on a specific deliverable). Use the real one or none.

## How we write instead (the SattvIQ voice)

- **Say what the thing is and what it does.** Do not say what it is not. ("P&IDs become structured, queryable data." Not "P&IDs as data. Not paper.")
- **Be specific.** Name the document, the standard, the number, the saved hours. Specificity is credibility, especially to engineers.
- **Earn every adjective.** Prefer a verb and a fact over an adjective. "Flags the line where the design pressure disagrees" beats "powerful, intelligent compliance".
- **Vary the rhythm.** Mix long and short sentences. Read it aloud; if it sounds like a brochure, cut it.
- **Lead with the reader's problem in their words.** EPC engineers talk about checking, re-checking, version control, deviations, man-hours, schedule. Use their language.
- **One claim, then the proof.** Not three claims and no proof.
- **No long dashes. Ever.** Hyphen, comma, colon, parentheses, or a full stop.

## A fast pre-publish checklist

Before anything goes out, scan for: long dashes, "not just / it's not just", rule-of-three lists, the words in B.12, em-dash-style interrupters, emoji bullets, "in today's / in the world of", a restated conclusion, and any sentence that names no specific thing. If you hit two or more, rewrite.
